What it is

Paddle Stroke is open firmware for the RAK WisMesh Tag, a small Meshtastic tracker with an accelerometer and Bluetooth. Fix the Tag firmly to the boat and it acts as a standard Bluetooth cycling cadence sensor. Each paddle stroke counts as one pedal turn, so a bike computer's cadence field shows your stroke rate.

It replaces Meshtastic entirely. While this firmware is on the Tag, it is no longer a tracker or a mesh node: the GPS is powered off, the LoRa radio is never used, and nothing is sent or received over the mesh. What's left is the accelerometer and Bluetooth. To get the tracker back, you flash stock Meshtastic again.

It was built and tested with a COOSPO CS300 head unit, first in an OC1 and with an OC6 as the goal. Any device that pairs with a Bluetooth cadence sensor should work the same way.

Status: experimental. The current firmware (m5_acf) works on the bench and is being tested on the water. Power saving isn't done yet.

Why the WisMesh Tag

A stroke sensor needs an accelerometer, Bluetooth and a battery, in a box that survives spray and rain. The Tag already has all of that, ready-made.

Sealed against spray
The case is closed at the factory and rated IP66: protected against rain, spray and strong water jets, but not rated for being underwater. Preferably put it inside a sealed plastic junction box (IP67 or better). Fix the box rigidly to the boat and the Tag rigidly inside it, so the Tag still feels the boat's motion. Otherwise, mount it where water runs off rather than pools, not where bilge water collects. Sealing is the hardest part of a homemade sensor.
Rechargeable
A 1000 mAh battery, far more than a session needs, charged through a magnetic USB cable. The same cable carries data for flashing.
Easy to flash and easy to undo
It uses the nRF52840 chip, the same one as common hobby boards, with drag-and-drop firmware updates. Flashing Meshtastic back turns it into a tracker again.
Cheap for what it is
Around €47 in Europe. A sealed DIY build costs about the same once you add a battery, charger and a good box. Some US shops ask much more.

Trade-offs

  • You pay for GPS and LoRa hardware this firmware doesn't use.
  • IP66 is not a submersion rating. A Tag that spends time underwater, or goes in with a huli, may let water in.
  • The case can't be opened without breaking the seal, so the battery can't be replaced.
  • Charging and flashing need the magnetic cable, so keep a spare.

From hull motion to a number

Everything runs on the Tag. There is no phone app and no calibration step.

  1. Sample the hull

    The LIS2DH accelerometer is read 100 times a second.

  2. Find the surge

    Gravity is removed and the Tag works out which horizontal direction the boat surges along, however it is mounted.

  3. Keep the paddling band

    A 0.5–2 Hz band-pass filter keeps the rhythm of strokes and drops slow swell and fast vibration.

  4. Track the rhythm

    A running autocorrelation picks the stroke period five times a second. Rates outside 24–110 per minute are not shown, so the display goes blank instead of guessing.

  5. Send it as cadence

    The Tag advertises as “Paddle Stroke” using the standard Cycling Speed and Cadence profile, and the head unit shows strokes per minute.

How well it works so far

Measured on two OC1 sessions and on the bench. Steady paddling compared with a watch, in 12-second windows:

2.4strokes/min mean absolute error
76%of windows within ±3 strokes/min
1.3strokes/min jitter, against 3.5 for simple peak counting

Known limits

  • It takes about 10 seconds of paddling before a rate appears.
  • After you stop, the display blanks in 4–8 seconds in calm water and can take up to 19 seconds in rough water.
  • Waves that roll at a paddling-like rhythm can't be told apart from strokes.
  • Very slow paddling, around 15–20 strokes per minute, is not reliably caught.

Get started

Use at your own risk. This is experimental firmware for a device it wasn't made for. Flashing it can leave your Tag unusable, erase your Meshtastic settings and keys, or drain the battery. Back up first and keep the steps to revert to stock at hand. There is no warranty.

Flashing this firmware removes Meshtastic. Until you flash it back, the Tag has no GPS, no LoRa and no mesh.

  1. Build the firmware. PlatformIO goes into a local virtual environment; nothing is installed system-wide. 
    git clone https://github.com/leoniedu/tagvaa
cd tagvaa
python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install platformio
.venv/bin/pio run -e m5_acf      # writes firmware/m5_acf.uf2
  2. Put the Tag in bootloader mode. From stock Meshtastic, run meshtastic --port /dev/cu.usbmodem* --enter-dfu. From this firmware, type dfu in the serial monitor or hold the front button for 5 seconds. A USB drive named RAK4631 appears.
  3. Check before you copy. Open INFO_UF2.TXT: the SoftDevice line must say S140 6.1.x. If it says 7.x, stop. Copy CURRENT.UF2 somewhere safe as a full backup.
  4. Flash. Copy firmware/m5_acf.uf2 onto the drive. The Tag reboots into the new firmware by itself.
  5. Pair. On your head unit, add a cadence sensor and pick “Paddle Stroke”. The green LED blinks once per stroke.

Hardware details, the data logger, analysis scripts and every test result are in the README.
